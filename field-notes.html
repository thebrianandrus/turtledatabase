FIELD NOTES

Strange adaptations, deep-time mysteries, and the stories behind the shells.

Deep Time

How the turtle got its shell

For a long time, the shell was one of evolution's great riddles. A string of fossil discoveries finally revealed the steps.

Read the note

The shell isn't a separate piece of armor. It's built from the turtle's own ribs and backbone, broadened and fused with bony plates in the skin. So how did that happen?

Eunotosaurus, a Permian reptile from about 260 million years ago, already had unusually broad, flattened ribs. Some researchers think those ribs first helped it dig — and only later became protection.

Pappochelys, from about 240 million years ago, adds widened ribs and a basket of belly bones. Then Odontochelys, around 220 million years old, arrives with a complete belly shell but only a partial top. By the time of Proganochelys, the full shell was finished — and the turtle body plan has barely changed since.

Rivers

Breathing through the back door

Some river turtles can stay underwater for remarkable lengths of time. Their secret is unusual — and a little undignified.

Read the note

Turtles breathe air with lungs, but some freshwater species can also absorb dissolved oxygen from the water through linings richly supplied with blood vessels — including in the cloaca, the shared opening at the rear of the body.

Australia's Mary River turtle is one of the most famous practitioners, able to stay submerged for very long periods in well-oxygenated water. Scientists informally call it 'cloacal respiration' — and everyone else calls it bum-breathing.

The adaptation lets these turtles hide from predators and conserve energy on the river bottom. It also makes them vulnerable: when rivers are dammed or polluted and oxygen levels fall, so do their chances.

Oceans

The map inside every hatchling

A sea turtle hatchling has never seen the ocean. Within minutes, it's navigating it.

Read the note

Hatchlings scramble toward the brightest horizon — the moonlit sea — then swim into the waves. Out there, they need to stay inside currents that keep them warm and fed.

Experiments with loggerhead hatchlings showed they respond to the magnetic signatures of specific places along their migration route, turning in the direction that keeps them on course. The magnetic field acts as both compass and map.

Years later, adult females use the same sense to return to the stretch of coast where they hatched. It's one of the most impressive feats of navigation in the animal kingdom.

Oceans

When temperature decides

In most sea turtles and many other turtles, there are no sex chromosomes to settle the question. The sand does.

Read the note

During a sensitive window of development, nest temperature determines whether an embryo becomes male or female. In sea turtles, warmer sand produces more females; cooler sand produces more males.

The difference between the two can be just a few degrees. That makes nesting beaches extraordinarily sensitive to climate — and some warm nesting regions already produce almost entirely female hatchlings.

Conservationists are experimenting with shading nests, relocating eggs and watering sand to cool it down. The long-term fix, of course, is a cooler planet.

Rivers

The worm that bites back

The alligator snapping turtle doesn't hunt. It fishes.

Read the note

Lying still on a river bottom with its jaws wide open, the alligator snapper wiggles a pink, worm-like appendage on its tongue. To a passing fish, it looks like a meal.

When the fish swims in for a closer look, the jaws slam shut. It's one of the very few examples of a reptile using a lure built into its own body.

The species can grow enormous and live for decades, but it was heavily harvested for turtle soup in the past century. It's now protected across much of its range.

Lands

Jonathan of St Helena

On a remote island in the South Atlantic lives a tortoise who was already old when the photograph was invented.

Read the note

Jonathan, a Seychelles giant tortoise, was brought to St Helena in 1882 and was already fully grown then. Based on that, he is believed to have hatched around 1832.

That makes him the oldest known living land animal. He has outlived dozens of the island's governors who lived in the house whose lawn he grazes.

Long lifespans like his are part of what makes tortoises vulnerable: they take many years to reach breeding age, so populations recover very slowly once adults are lost.
